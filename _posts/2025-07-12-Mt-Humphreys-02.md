This part of the hike was a stark contrast to everything we had seen so far. It felt as though the mountain had "leveled up" and become an entirely different beast. The lush greens had given way to grim grays and fleshy pinks of exposed rock. The trail was no longer straightforward; finding the safest way upward often felt like solving a small puzzle. Dirt and tree roots had been replaced by stones and loose rocks, and we had to watch our footing carefully.

We saw two or three people slip on their way down. Each time, my heart dropped at the thought of something similar happening to someone in our group. I tried to bring my attention back to the present. "One step at a time," I reminded myself, recalling advice from one of the more experienced hikers with us.

Soon, my friend and I caught up with the rest of the group, who had overtaken us despite our head start and were now taking a break. I put on my hoodie against the cold wind. Hikers descending the mountain kept encouraging us: we were "nearly there," they said, and the view was "totally worth it." Those brief exchanges helped more than I expected. In Part 1, I wrote about finding my own pace. That still mattered, but so did the companionship along the way. We each had our own struggle, yet a shared goal made it easy to offer a few words of encouragement.

The question "Why?" can become surprisingly difficult when I'm tired or discouraged. A decision that once seemed obvious starts to invite doubt. Sometimes another person helps by expressing something I already know in just the right way. Hearing a familiar thought articulated clearly can be a powerful thing. At other times, encouragement helps by drawing my attention away from the larger question and back to the next step. It doesn't resolve everything, but it can give me enough momentum to keep going. On that mountain, the kindness of passing hikers did exactly that.

Throughout this stretch, we kept our pace under control, as we had decided more than two miles earlier. The altitude wasn't troubling me as much now, and I was relieved. I paused occasionally to admire the view, but still resisted taking it all in. I wanted to save that for the summit. Which, surely, was only another 15 or 20 minutes away. The relief was immense. In my eagerness, I sped up. I had nearly climbed Mt. Humphreys, the tallest peak in Arizona! Then, as I approached the apparent summit, I began to see what lay behind it.

My heart sank. This wasn't the summit. It was a false peak, hiding the rest of the mountain and the true summit beyond it. I was devastated. Other hikers had left me with the impression that I was practically done with this damn mountain. Yet there it stood, still looming over me. How much farther could it possibly go?

All of this was happening in my head. Surrounded by friends and strangers, my feet had continued climbing almost of their own accord. I tried once more to focus on the next step. The mountain had got me, but I couldn't bear to give up after coming so far. I looked back at the trail and the treeline below, reminding myself of the progress I had already made. We kept going.
