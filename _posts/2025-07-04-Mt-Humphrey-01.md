My heart was pounding at a solid 142 bpm as my lungs fought for every last molecule of oxygen. I walked faster, trying to keep up with the group, and glanced at the mileage tracker on my watch. "0.76 mi," read the display. It took a second for that number to register. When it did, I found myself sincerely questioning my decision to attempt this hike. I was in no physical shape to make the climb. The out-and-back distance was nearly 10 miles, much of it above 10,000 feet. I was beginning to realize how badly I had underestimated the altitude. Every breath was a battle.

I looked to my companions for reassurance. Unfortunately, they seemed to be doing just fine, marching their way up this ginormous mountain. Later that day, I learned that I wasn't the only one struggling. At the time, though, it was easy to believe that everyone else had it figured out. It's remarkable how quickly the mind can turn that comparison into permission to quit: *They're fitter than you. They've done this before.* I began to convince myself that my struggle was somehow exceptional, and that anyone in my shoes would want to turn back. It seemed pretty logical in the moment.

Ironically, the very thing causing me so much worry was also what kept me going. Had I been hiking alone, I would almost certainly have turned around at 0.76 miles. But I had too much pride to admit to the group that this might be beyond me, especially before the first mile. These were people I would see at work for years. Whether or not they would think any less of me, I knew I would struggle to face them after giving up so early. And so I continued fighting my way up that dastardly mountain.

We took breaks every mile or so, refueling with nuts, electrolytes, and plenty of water. The first mile had been the hardest on my lungs; the altitude had caught them completely off guard. I had expected breathing to become steadily harder as we climbed and was sure I'd pass out by mile three. Fortunately, my lungs began to adjust. Between miles two and three, things became difficult again, but for a different reason. The trail looked deceptively gentle, tempting me to walk faster while quietly gaining elevation. As a novice hiker, I fell for the trap and tired myself out unnecessarily.

By the three-mile mark, my friend and I had realized that the key was to find our own pace. Everyone on the trail was climbing the same mountain, but each person was having a different experience. Watching someone else move faster didn't tell me much about what my own pace should be. The battle was against the mountain, not the other hikers. Perhaps even that was partly a matter of perspective. But on that particular day, the mountain certainly felt like my enemy.

Somewhere between miles three and four, we cleared the treeline and reached a signpost pointing toward Humphreys Peak. We could already see what looked like the summit, and our spirits lifted. The terrain had become steeper and rockier, but at least we were nearly done. The view was already breathtaking. I didn't want to dwell on it, though; I was determined to save that moment for the top. While some of the group stopped to take pictures by the signpost, my friend and I continued at our carefully chosen pace. We were slower than several of the others and wanted to give ourselves a head start.
