My heart was pounding a solid 142bpm as my lungs fought for every last molecule of oxygen in every breath. 
I walked faster trying to keep up with the group as I glanced at my watch's mileage tracker -- "0.76mi", read the display. 
It took a second for that number to register in my mind and as it did, I found myself sincerely questioning my decision to attempt this hike. 
I was in no physical shape to make this climb. The out-and-back distance of the Humphreys peak hike was nearly 10 miles, most of which occurs at an elevation above ~10,000ft. 
The impact of this later part, I was starting to realize, I had grossly underestimated. Every breathe was a battle entirely unto itself. 
Humans are social creatures afterall, and so I looked to my companions for reassurance and support. I hate evolution for giving me that instinct to gaze around 
me because they all seemed to be doing just fine, if not completely in their stride, essentially marching their way up this ginormous mountain. 
Later on that day I discovered that I wasn't the only one struggling to cope with the sudden task of hiking at such an altitude but that just goes to 
show the kinds of tricks our minds play on us. We are inclined to make our suffering feel special -- something exclusive to ourselves -- and thus, make it a "valid" 
reason to give up and give in. ("They are more fit than you", "They are more experienced hikers", and what not.) We give ourselves the impression
that we are going through something that no one else has or will ever experience, and so, if anyone else were in your shoes they too would quit. Therefore, 
why shouldn't you? Seems pretty logical in the moment. 
Ironically, in this setting, the very thing causing me angst and worry was what kept me going. Had I been going up that mountain by myself, I certainly would 
have turned back at 0.76 miles. Fortunately or unfortunately, I had too much pride to accept to the rest of these people that this hike may be too much for me 
(come on, what kind of a whuss gives up before even the 1mi mark?). These were people that I would 
see for at least a couple years in my workplace and whether they would think ill of me or not, I knew that if I tapped out this early, I would never be able to face them
again. For that sole reason, I continued to fight my way up that dastardly mountain. 

We took breaks every mile or so, and refilled on calories with nuts, electrolytes and of course, plenty of water. Mile one was the hardest on my lungs as they really
caught off-guard by the rate at which they were expected to handle the respiratory system. My initial expectation was that the stress on my lungs would increase 
roughly linearly with my gained elevation and so I expected myself to pass out by mile three for sure. Luckily that didn't happen and my lungs got more accustomed to
the altitude with time. Between miles two and three, things got difficult yet again but for different reasons; this turned out to be a theme for the whole hike. Here, 
the trail looked simple, thus tempting me into walking at a higher pace but in a very subtle manner the trail had gained inclination making each step much harder. 
The novice hiker such as myself fell for this trap, tiring myself out unnecessarily. By the time I reached the 3mi mark, me and my closest friend from our group realized
that the key to tackling this beast of a hike was to do it at our own pace. Every person on this trail was attempting to conquer this great mountain, fighting their own
inner demons on their own personal version of this quest. From one person's perspective, it may seem that the other was moving faster or "making progress" more quickly,
but it is crucial to remind ourselves in those moments where we compare ourselves with others that this is a solitary journey. The battle is against the mountain not against
others. I would go so far as to say that even the mountain is merely a projection of our mind, as it is what we make of it. Having said that, today, the mountain seemed my enemy. 
At some point between miles three and four, we cleared the treeline and reached a signpost pointing us further towards Humphreys peak. As we were walking up to the signpost
we had already seen the summit and were starting to feel much better about ourselves. Yes, the terrain got much worse due to loose rocks and steeper inclination but at least we 
were nearly done. Needless to say the view was already breathtaking but I didn't want to dwell on it too much as I was deadset on enjoying the view only once I made it to the top. 
Some of the other members in the group were taking pictures with the signpost already, but my friend and me continued onwards maintaining our pace and making sure not to rush ourselves
(we were afterall slower than some of the others in the group and so wanted to give ourselves a headstart).
