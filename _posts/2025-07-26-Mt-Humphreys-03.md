The cold wind had my nose running. My quads were beginning to burn from the steeper climb and larger rocks, while my lungs were hanging in there thanks to the slower pace. I had just passed the second false peak, and my heart was drowning in misery. The rest of my body seemed to be carrying that extra weight too. It felt as though we had been climbing forever, certainly beyond the five miles I had expected for the ascent. After two false peaks, I no longer trusted the next apparent summit. Not knowing how much farther we had to go made it harder to stay focused on the present.

A definite endpoint can be a powerful source of motivation. At the gym, it might be "Just one more set" or "Three more reps." Knowing that the effort will end helps me push through it. But what happens when the finish line keeps moving out of sight?

Fortunately, I heard other hikers mention that there were only two false peaks. Where had these people been before the first one? This time, we really were approaching the summit. As I climbed, I spotted the trail marker identifying Humphreys Peak and its elevation of 12,633 feet. My heart began to lighten, and I felt an almost immediate surge of energy.

A few minutes later, I was standing on the summit. My first major hike: done!

At last, I let myself take in the panoramic view. It stretched to the horizon in every direction, with nothing nearby blocking it. We could see Flagstaff to the south and glimpse the rim of the Grand Canyon to the north. It was stunning, and well worth the arduous climb. I sat down and tried to get comfortable. For a moment, I felt compelled to acknowledge the mountain itself, almost as though we had finished a duel. I was grateful to the friends who had invited me, to the hikers who had encouraged us, and, somehow, to the mountain for standing the test of time and waiting for me. The whole experience felt surreal and deeply satisfying.

Then I noticed something crawling up my shoulder. I brushed it off, only to discover a few more on my other shoulder. My friend pointed out that there were some in my hair too. Eww. I ruffled my hair briskly, thoroughly unsettled by the feeling of the bugs coming loose. There was a massive swarm around us. They looked like fruit flies, and I later learned they were gnats. I wasn't exactly panicking, but I certainly didn't want to remain a stationary target.

I got up and walked around. One of the others was trying to photograph a bird that looked like a raven, high above us, surfing a wave of wind. Here we were, more than 12,600 feet up, feeling immensely pleased with what we had accomplished. Yet for that bird, being at this height was just another Monday. That put things in perspective.
